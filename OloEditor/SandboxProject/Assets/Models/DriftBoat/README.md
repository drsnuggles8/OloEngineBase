# DriftBoat: the boat you sail in Drift

A small Nordic clinker boat with one square sail: double-ended, seven lapped strakes a side, a low
plank deck with a raised deck at each end, a pole mast stepped a little aft of amidships, and a side
rudder on the starboard quarter. It is 7.5 m overall and 2.6 m in the beam, and it displaces the
2600 kg the game's rigid body has always had.

She has no thwarts. A decked boat of this kind is braced by crossbeams at the gunwale (three here,
the mast beam with hanging knees under it) and her crew sits on the sea chests, which are on the
deck with the rest of the gear; the oars work against thole pins on the gunwale.

It is an original design. Its shapes are those of the working boats of the Nordic clinker tradition
in general (lapped planking riveted through roves, curved stems carried above the sheer, a square
sail on a yard, a steering oar); it copies no particular ship and no film's or game's boat. Every
file here is generated by `build_drift_boat.py` in this repository.

## Why a square rig

`SailComponent` models one sail on a yard that braces up to 45 degrees either side: the boat reaches
and runs, cannot point closer than about 50 degrees to the wind, and changes tack by wearing round
(put through the wind she stops in irons). A square sail on a braced yard is
that model drawn literally, so the sail on screen and the force in the physics are the same thing.
A fore-and-aft rig would need a different sail model. The 45 degree limit is also real on this boat:
the shrouds are led well aft so the yard can swing that far and no further.

## Files

| file | what it is |
|---|---|
| `build_drift_boat.py` | the Blender 5.x script that writes everything below, from nothing |
| `DriftBoat.gltf` + `.bin` | the hull, decks, mast, standing rigging and deck gear: one mesh, 30 860 triangles |
| `DriftBoat_LOD1.gltf`, `_LOD2.gltf` | the same boat at 8 961 and 2 270 triangles |
| `DriftBoatSail.gltf` (+ `_LOD1`, `_LOD2`) | the yard and the sail, origined on the mast axis: 15 492, 2 766 and 360 triangles, each with the ten morph targets the cloth is blended from |
| `DriftBoatRudder.gltf` (+ `_LOD1`) | the side rudder and its tiller, origined on the shaft at the gunwale |
| `DriftBoatLine.gltf` | one metre of rope along +Z, which the scene stretches into each sheet and brace |
| `DriftBoatHullColor.png`, `…Normal.png`, `…ORM.png` | 4096² each: the hull's material, at 337 texels a metre (3 mm a texel) |
| `DriftBoatRigColor.png`, `…Normal.png`, `…ORM.png` | 2048² each: the material the sail, rudder and line share |
| `DriftBoat.rig.json` | everything measured: the hull's hydrostatics, the sail's area and centre, where the parts hang, the rigid body the hull implies (`physics`), and each level of detail's silhouette score |
| `*_LOD0.olomesh`, `_LOD1`, `_LOD2` | the engine's Mesh assets a `LODGroupComponent` level names; each is two lines pointing at one glTF |

Units are metres, Y up, the bow faces +Z and **+X is port** (the engine's starboard is forward × up,
which for a +Z bow is −X). The origin is not the keel: **y = 0 is the waterline** the hull floats at
when it displaces 2600 kg, and **z = 0 is its centre of buoyancy**. The scene hangs the hull mesh
below the physics entity by exactly the height that entity floats at, with no scale, so the model's
waterline is the sea. Every glTF has one node with an identity transform.

## The contract with the scene

`Assets/Scenes/Drift.olo` draws the boat as fourteen entities under the physics root `Boat`. The scene's
comments are the design record for the numbers; this is the part the model owns.

| entity | draws | its transform |
|---|---|---|
| `Boat Hull` | `DriftBoat.gltf` | translation Y = `physics.hullMeshOffsetY`: the physics entity's origin floats that far above the waterline |
| `Boat Sail` | `DriftBoatSail.gltf` | translation = the mast (`sail.origin`); **its Y rotation is the yard angle**, written every tick. It carries a `MorphTargetComponent`, whose weights are written every tick too |
| `Boat Rudder` | `DriftBoatRudder.gltf` | translation = `rudder.pivot`; Z rotation = the cant (`rudder.cantDeg`), authored; **Y rotation is the helm**, written every tick |
| `Boat Sheet Port` / `Starboard`, `Boat Brace Port` / `Starboard` | `DriftBoatLine.gltf` | translation = the line's lead on the gunwale (`lines.*`); rotation and Z scale are written every tick |
| `Boat Clew Port` / `Starboard`, `Boat Yardarm Port` / `Starboard` | nothing | children of the sail, at `sail.clew*` and `sail.yardArm*`: where each line's far end is |
| `Boat Clew Port Furled` / `Starboard Furled` | nothing | children of the sail, at `sail.clew*Furled`: where each clew is with the sail gathered right up to the yard. A sheet ends between its two markers, as far along as the sail is shortened; the markers do not move |

`DriftBoatController.lua` finds these by name. It copies `SailComponent.yardAngle` onto the sail,
blends the cloth's shape from the wind, turns the rudder with the helm, and stretches each line from
its lead to its marker. The engine
composes an entity's Euler rotation X first, then Y, then Z, which is why one entity can carry the
rudder's cant (Z) and its helm (Y): the blade turns about its own canted shaft.

**Materials.** Two, each one mesh's only material: `DriftBoatHull` and `DriftBoatRig`. Each has a
base colour map, a tangent-space normal map (OpenGL convention, +Y up) and one map carrying
occlusion in red, roughness in green and metallic in blue. Nothing is double-sided: the engine does
not flip the normal of a back face, so the sail is two faces a few millimetres apart and every
plank is a solid.

**Levels of detail.** The parts are drawn through `MeshComponent` with an authored
`LODGroupComponent`, because `ModelComponent` has no levels of detail. The engine swaps one submesh
for another and shades it with level 0's material, so each file is a single mesh and **every level
lands on the same texels**: the generator rebuilds the boat with fewer stations along its length and
drops what is too small to see, and places each coarser part on the chart its full version painted.
Past 90 m the standing rigging is drawn thicker on purpose: a line under a pixel wide shimmers.

The generator checks its own work, before it writes a file. It draws each level's silhouette from
the side, the end and above on a grid of one pixel at the distance the level takes over (30 m and
90 m, at the game camera's field of view and 1080 lines), and stops if a level of the hull, the sail
or the rudder overlaps its level 0 by less than 0.90. The scores are in `DriftBoat.rig.json`
(`levelsOfDetail`); the lowest today is 0.925.

A `.olomesh` names its glTF by asset handle, and handles are minted by the project's asset registry,
so those eight files are the only ones here the script does not write. They do not change when the
boat is regenerated. If a glTF is ever added or renamed, register it (open the project in the
editor, or `olo_asset_import`), read its handle back (`olo_assets_list`), and write the two lines.

**The cloth.** The sail's files hold it full of a moderate breeze, and ten morph targets
(`sail.morphTargets` in the rig file, in file order):

| target | the sail |
|---|---|
| `Slack` | hanging from the yard in a calm, in loose vertical folds |
| `Hard` | bellied out by a gale |
| `Aback` | pressed aft round the mast by wind on its fore face |
| `Furled` | gathered right up to the yard |
| `Luff0`, `Luff120`, `Luff240` | one slow, deep wave at three phases a third of a cycle apart |
| `Ripple0`, `Ripple120`, `Ripple240` | one quick, shallow wave, the same way |

The script blends them from `SailComponent`: apparent wind speed sets how full she is, the side the
wind is on chooses between filling and lying aback, `luffing` shakes her, and `sailSetInput`
(including the automatic reef in a storm) gathers her up, with the sheets following the clews. A
travelling wave is three standing ones because a morph weight cannot be negative: with weights
`(1 + cos(wt - k x 120 deg)) / 2` their steady parts cancel and a wave runs through the cloth.
This is drawing only. The force on the boat is `SailSystem`'s and does not read the cloth.

The engine names an imported target by its index (`MorphTarget_0` on), not by the name in the file,
so the script lists the names in file order and `DriftBoatDelivery` checks that list against the rig
file. Each target is the whole sail rebuilt in another shape, so the bolt rope and the reef points
move with the cloth, and every level of detail carries all ten.

## How it is made

**The hull is a function of its parameters** (`HULL` in the script): length, beam, depth, the rise
of the sheer at each end, the rocker of the keel, the rake of the stems, the entry and exit angles,
deadrise, flare, the firmness of the bilge, and the number, widths, thickness and overlap of the
strakes. From those it builds the sheer in profile and in plan, the keel and the two stems as one
backbone, and a cross-section at every station.

**The planking is laid, not drawn.** Each strake is a solid plank, flat across its width, its lower
edge lying on the outside of the strake below by one plank thickness. The lap fades out toward the
ends, where a real boatbuilder cuts gains so the planks come flush at the stem. The strakes are
lined off in girth, and each land ends where the rising stem meets it, so the hood ends climb the
stem in turn. That is why the laps show in the silhouette and catch the light.

**It is floated before it is exported.** The script integrates the hull's volume below a trial
waterline, finds the waterline at which that is 2.6 m³, and moves the origin onto it and onto the
centre of buoyancy. Draft, freeboard, waterline length and beam in the rig file are measurements of
that hull, not inputs.

**Everything is a grid.** Each surface is a grid of points with its own arc lengths as texture
coordinates, so a plank's grain runs along the plank and the rivets sit on the lap. Each grid is one
chart of the atlas, packed at one texel density (fewer texels for what the deck hides). The glTF
files and the PNGs are written by the script itself, byte for byte the same on every run.

**The maps are painted per chart, in metres.** For every texel the painter knows where on the boat
it is, which way it faces and how occluded it is, so the slime sits below the waterline, the salt
just above it, the bleaching on what faces the sky, and the worn wood where feet and hands go. Any
detail finer than the chart's texels can carry is dropped rather than left to alias.

**Blender does two things:** it traces the ambient occlusion (Cycles, on the CPU, into the atlas),
and it renders the previews. This departs from baking Blender shader nodes: painting in the script
is what lets a feature be placed in a plank's own coordinates, and it is how the dog's colour maps
are made too.

## The rigid body

`DriftBoat.rig.json`'s `physics` block is the body `Drift.olo` authors, derived by
`physics_from_hull()`:

- the **collider** is the hull as a box (waterline beam and length, keel to gunwale), offset to
  where the hull is drawn. It is what touches bottom when she grounds. Jolt takes the body's
  **centre of mass** and inertia from it, so the centre of mass is the middle of the hull, 2 cm
  from her waterline;
- the **buoyancy box** is centred on the entity and has the hull's half-beam and half its waterline
  length. Its half-height is the one free number. A box carrying this mass floats with its bottom
  corners `d = mass × ramp / (4 × density × X × Y × Z)` under water. Rolled a little, those corners
  right her with `weight × X² / d` per radian, less the weight times the height of the centre of
  mass above them. Y is solved to make that stiffness, divided by the height the sail's force acts
  at above the centre of mass, the value the game was tuned at. She then lies over as far in a
  given wind as the boat before her did (1.1° in the 5 m/s breeze, 4.5° in the 14 m/s gale, against
  1.2° and 4.4°);
- the entity's origin is therefore neither her centre of mass nor her waterline: that box floats
  it `Y − d` above the sea. The **hull mesh hangs that far below it**, the boat is authored at
  that height in the scene, and every offset on the components is measured from it;
- the **centre of effort's height** is not the drawn sail's either. `SailArea` is a game number,
  twenty times the canvas that is drawn, and the height it acts at is the lever that lays her over.
  The drawn sail's centre is 2.82 m above the centre of mass; the game was tuned with 2.0 m on a
  1.35 m half-beam. With the force at the drawn centre she heeled 1.7 times as far. With the box
  stiffened to cancel that, she heeled the same in a steady wind and still spent 1.6 times as long
  past ten degrees when a storm overpowered her (37% of a beam reach against 23%, six runs each),
  because the side force that lifts the windward bilge clear is `weight × half-beam / lever` and
  nothing else. So the lever is `GAME_FEEL["heel_lever"]` half-beams (1.93 m), which keeps that
  force at 17 kN. The measured centre stays in the rig file (`sail.centreOfArea`,
  `physics.drawnSailAboveCentreOfMass`);
- the **centre of effort's station** is not the drawn sail's. `BoatSystem` resists leeway at the
  centre of mass, where a real hull under way resists it some way forward of that, and what turns
  a boat is the distance between the two. The force is put where it turns this body into the wind
  as hard, per newton, as the game was tuned for (`GAME_FEEL["weather_helm"]` times the body's yaw
  inertia: 0.50 m abaft the centre of mass). That is the drawn sail over a centre of lateral
  resistance 0.30 m, 5% of the waterline, forward of the centre of mass
  (`physics.lateralResistanceLead`). With the force at the drawn sail's own station a storm gust
  bore her away where it used to round her up;
- the **thrust point** is the foot of the rudder blade: on this boat the auxiliary is the steering
  oar sculled as a sweep, and the rudder bites until its blade is out of the water;
- the **damping and rudder coefficients** are whatever keeps the rest of the feel the game was
  tuned to (`GAME_FEEL` in the script: surge damping, roll decay, the helm's time constant and turn
  rate, how deep the hull must sit for its drag to bite) true for this box.

`DriftBoatDelivery.TheSceneIsBuiltFromTheGeneratedRig` fails if the scene, the rig file and the
presets the sailing tests use disagree.

## Regenerating

From the repository root, with Blender 5.x:

```
blender -b --factory-startup --python OloEditor/SandboxProject/Assets/Models/DriftBoat/build_drift_boat.py
```

A second argument after `--` names the output directory (default: this one), and a third a
directory for preview renders:

```
blender -b --factory-startup --python OloEditor/SandboxProject/Assets/Models/DriftBoat/build_drift_boat.py -- OloEditor/SandboxProject/Assets/Models/DriftBoat build/boat-preview
```

A full build takes about two minutes, most of it the occlusion trace. For quick shape work,
`OLO_BOAT_ATLAS=1024 OLO_BOAT_SAIL_ATLAS=512 OLO_BOAT_AO_SAMPLES=8` brings it under twenty seconds.

After a change to the hull, the sail or the rudder, the numbers in `Drift.olo`'s boat block and in
`OloEngine/tests/DriftScenePresets.h` have to follow `DriftBoat.rig.json`; the delivery test names
each one that has not. Then run the sailing suites, which sail this exact body in the game's three
winds:

```
OloEngine-Tests --gtest_filter=DriftBoatDelivery*:DriftSailingTest.*:SailTest.*
```

The previews are a shape check. The look is judged in the engine: open `Scenes/Drift.olo`.
